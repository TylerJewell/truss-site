**Prepared by Tyler Jewell for Fred Prosser, Todd Fieken, and Ike Isakson** · July 2026

---

I want to buy Truss Components of Oregon, and I want to run it the way you've run it. You'd keep 20%. This is my thinking on where I'd take the business and what I believe it can earn.

## 1. The company today

Here's what it has going for it:

- About 150 customers across Oregon and Washington who count on you to deliver on time and get it right.
- Every truss engineered to load and stamped by a licensed Professional Engineer.
- Foremen and sawyers who've been on the floor 15, 20, even 30 years. People don't leave.
- Your own trucks and a 22-ton crane, so you deliver and set the trusses yourselves, right on the plate line.
- Forty years of bids in the Alpine system: every job you laid out, what it cost, what you charged, and whether you won it.

I wouldn't touch any of it.

## 2. What I'd leave alone

- The quality, and the promise to hit the delivery date.
- The crew. If anything, I'd give everyone who works here a share of the ownership.
- The engineering, and the PE stamp on every job.
- The name. I'm not putting a new sign out front.

## 3. How contractors choose a supplier

When a contractor picks a truss supplier, price usually isn't what decides it. A few things matter more:

- **Delivery on time.** A late load of trusses leaves the framing crew standing around. Contractors also want a straight answer when a date is going to slip.
- **Getting the design right.** A mistake turns into a fix in the field and a backcharge.
- **Drawings the building department will sign off on.** PE-stamped and to code.
- **A little help up front.** Catch a framing problem in the plans and you save the contractor money.
- **A quote that comes back fast and is right.**

You already do all of this.

The big suppliers — Parr, Builders FirstSource, Pacific Lumber — have the scale, but they're built for volume, and a small or custom or ag job goes to the back of the line. The smaller shops — Oregon Truss, Precision, Ariel — have the relationships, but their websites are thin, you can't get a quote online, and none of them show their credentials. You can do the engineering and the delivery and still be easy to get a quote out of. Nobody else in this market does both.

Getting in front of more contractors takes two things:

- **Reach.** Widen and maximize exposure to every general contractor in Oregon and Washington, through a website, a salesperson calling on general contractors, and a presence in the commercial plan rooms.
- **Bidding.** Right now every quote gets built by hand, so there's a ceiling on how many jobs you can go after. Software that drafts the quote off your own past bids lets the same estimators put out a lot more of them, without getting sloppy.

You still build trusses the same way. When there's more work, you add a second shift, and the equipment for it is already here.

## 4. How I'd grow it

Here's what I'd do:

- **Market the place.** A website, and outreach to more builders.
- **Speed up the bidding.** Software that drafts quotes off your own history, so the estimators get more out the door. The estimator and the PE still sign off on every one.
- **Go straight to the general contractors.** Most of the commercial work comes in through framers today. I'd add the GCs directly and keep the framers too.
- **Run a second shift once the work is there.** The plant ran two shifts for years, and the equipment's still here for it. I'd bring the shift back once there's enough work to keep it busy.
- **Look at Bend, Salem, or Eugene down the road.** Buy a small shop or add capacity out there when the timing's right.
- **Give the crew a stake.** People who own a piece of the place tend to stick around.
- **Get the estimating knowledge into a system,** so a new estimator prices a job the way the shop always has.
